1. Why there is no ranking#

A composite score is only as good as its weights, and there is no outside standard to justify them. Public performance records are also concentrated in large US orchestras and the Seoul Philharmonic. The Vienna and Berlin Philharmonics and the Concertgebouw have searchable concert archives on their websites, but we found no public bulk download or API for the full record, so on this page they appear only through their Carnegie Hall concerts.

Critics' polls exist (Gramophone 2008, Bachtrack 2015 and 2023), but the Bachtrack polls reflect the views of 16 (2015) and 15 (2023) critics. This page sets out one comparison table per measure instead.

3. Can the Carnegie Hall records be trusted?#

We measured the same orchestra from two sources. Composer shares for the New York Philharmonic were computed from its own archive and from the Carnegie Hall records, and compared by cosine similarity (1 means identical distributions). In the 1890s–1960s, covering the years Carnegie Hall was the orchestra's home (1892–1962), the two agree closely (0.92–0.99). After the move to Lincoln Center in 1962 its Carnegie appearances shrink to a few special concerts and the similarity falls to 0.24–0.64. Carnegie figures on this page therefore describe what an orchestra played at Carnegie Hall.

6. Seoul and New York — subscription concerts 2009–2025#

Both orchestras' subscription concerts are matched by concert date (2009–2025). New York seasons start in September, so its programs are selected by first concert date rather than by season. Of 1,586 Seoul Philharmonic works, 1,362 (85.9%) have an attributed composer. The New York Philharmonic has 4,913 subscription work performances; 66 entries that do not name a single composer (traditional, anthems, unknown, non-works, and joint credits such as "Perlmutter / Wohl") are left out.

  • The repertoire is very similar. Cosine 0.93.
  • Seoul leans more on its top composers. Seoul's low end, 39.7%, is above the top of the NY range, 38.6%, so the order holds however the unattributed works fall. The gap is 1.1 percentage points, so it is sensitive to attribution errors.
  • Canceled and changed concerts do not change this. The Seoul data include concerts that were canceled or whose program changed. There are 63 subscription entries for 2020, against 37 to 52 in the other years from 2015 to 2025. Without 2020 and 2021, Seoul's low end is 40.8% and the top of the NY range 39.0%, so the gap widens.
  • No verdict on living composers. Seoul's range is wide and contains the NY value.

How composers were attached

Program strings were split into works, then attributed in this order: a catalog number (BWV, K., D. and so on); an exact match to a Carnegie Hall title; then reference titles sharing genre, number, opus and key, used only when they point to one composer. A title with its own distinctive words must share one with the reference titles, and a stated key must match. What the rules cannot settle goes to a hand-made table. Bare genre titles such as "Violin Concerto", and titles with more than one candidate composer, were left unattributed.

7. Korean orchestras' own subscription programs — Seoul, Suwon, Bucheon and Busan#

At a glance

What this section looks at
Which composers the Korean orchestras that appear at the Symphony Festival put on their own subscription programs, compared for the four orchestras whose programs could be read as text. The four are compared over 2009–2021, the years all four sources cover; the two long series, Suwon Phil and Busan Phil, are also set out by decade.
What shows
Cut to 223 works each, the ten most programmed composers take 68.2% at the Suwon Phil, 54.7% at the Bucheon Phil, 52.8% at the Busan Phil and 49.9% at the Seoul Phil. The closest pair by composer shares is Suwon Phil and Busan Phil (cosine 0.92). Beethoven is the most programmed composer at all four.
Watch for
The sources differ in kind and in the years they cover: programs announced on concert pages for Suwon and Bucheon, public concert data for Seoul, and the orchestra's own record book for Busan. The tables set measures side by side; they are not a ranking.

The figures for the Suwon Philharmonic Orchestra and the Bucheon Philharmonic Orchestra come from the past-concert pages on the two orchestras' websites. Those for the Busan Philharmonic Orchestra come from the subscription-concert tables of the orchestra's own record book, 부산시립교향악단 공연보 (1962–2021), a booklet posted on the orchestra's pages of the Busan Cultural Center website. For the Seoul Philharmonic Orchestra we use the same public data as in section 6, restricted to orchestral subscription concerts (the chamber series is left out). The Suwon Phil site lists subscription concerts from 1982, and programs appear as text through the first half of 2024; later pages show the program only as an image, which we did not transcribe. Subscription concerts in the Bucheon Phil archive start in 2006; the Busan Phil record book runs from the first subscription concert in 1962 to 2021.

The unit is one work on one concert program. A program given on two days counts once per listed concert. Concerts marked canceled or postponed are left out. Composer names follow the New York Philharmonic performance history, as elsewhere on this site; a name missing from that list is counted as one under the spelling in the source. The number of composers below is therefore a number of names as written, not of persons (a group counts as one, and a joint work goes to the first name given). The Suwon, Bucheon and Seoul data are announced programs and the Busan data are the orchestra's own record; none of them has been checked against what was actually played.

Why cut to the same number of works. The top-10 share and the number of composers move mechanically with sample size (fewer works bring fewer composers). So we cut the other three orchestras to the 223 works of the Suwon Phil, the smallest sample, by drawing 223 works at random 500 times from their works with a known composer and reporting the mean and the middle 95% of the draws (the random seed, the starting value of the draws, is fixed at 0 so the result can be reproduced). The bracketed values are that range. A wide range means the value depends a good deal on which works happen to be drawn. The range shows how much the value moves when works are redrawn from the table we have; it is not a confidence interval for an orchestra's whole repertory, and matching the number of works does not correct for missing programs or for the different nature of the sources.

The range for living composers. This is the share of works whose composer was alive in the concert year. Birth and death years come from Wikidata, a public knowledge base; the share cannot be pinned down with only the composers whose dates we know, so it is given as a range: the low end assumes every composer without known dates had died, the high end assumes they were all alive. Names without dates are those not found in the reference list and those found there but without dates in Wikidata (for Seoul, works without an attributed composer are included too). Some of them are living, so the true value may lie above the low end, and a low end of 0% does not mean that no living composer was programmed. A concert in the year of a composer's death counts as within the composer's lifetime. The four ranges overlap, so we draw no conclusion from this measure.

What counts as a subscription concert at the Bucheon Phil. The Bucheon Phil also numbers its New Year's and civic gala concerts as subscription concerts. In 2009–2021 there are 5 subscription concerts with nine or more works on the program, 68 works in all (19% of the 355 Bucheon Phil works). These programs carry many waltzes and polkas, so Johann Strauss II takes 8.2% at the Bucheon Phil, against 0.0% at the Seoul Phil, 0.4% at the Suwon Phil and 3.8% at the Busan Phil. The label "subscription concert" does not cover the same range of concerts at every orchestra.

7.8 How the tables were made

From the program block of each Suwon and Bucheon concert page, and from the program column of the Busan record book's tables, every line that starts with a composer's name became one work. Pages that run the whole program on one line were split by work, and pages that give a Korean and an English line for the same work were merged. Movement lines, intermission lines, performer lines and notices were not counted. Across the three orchestras and all kinds of concert, 10,264 works carry a name in the composer's place, and for 8,764 of them (85.4%) the name was matched to the New York Philharmonic list. The other 1,500 carry names this procedure could not match to that list and keep the name as written in the source (Korean composers and film and musical composers who are not in the list, names that are in it but that we could not match, and names written where a composer would be that may not be a composer's, such as singers and bands, or the word Anonymous). 522 lines that give a title without a composer, or whose composer could not be read, stay in the works table but enter no figure (such lines were not carried over exhaustively). Works and composers were taken only from what the sources say; nothing was filled in from elsewhere.

How names were matched, and the limits. A name written in full was matched to the person of that name in the New York Philharmonic records. A family name alone was matched automatically only to the person who appears most often under that name in the New York Philharmonic records, and only if that person appears there at least 20 times (the 50 composers whose Korean names this site had already fixed were matched regardless). Other spellings were matched only after checking the title by hand (the table orch_kr_composer_alias.csv, on the Data page). Names shared by several composers (Strauss, Weber and Webber, Williams and others) were separated by words in the title, and left as written when that failed. A match says whom the name in the source refers to; it is not a check of who actually wrote the piece. Some lines could not be read by these rules, such as a name missing from the list with no separator before the title, or a misspelled name. Conversely, the rules read some words as a composer that are a title, a genre label or a performer credit in the source (a line that gives the title before the composer, or one that begins with "film" or with a singer's name followed by "sung by"). We went through every spelling that was not matched to a New York Philharmonic name and listed 65 such words by hand (orch_kr_not_composer.csv, on the Data page). Of the 71 lines read with them, the 68 that carry a title are kept as lines whose composer could not be read, and the 3 notices were dropped. Other such words may remain. 138 concerts in the Bucheon Phil archive that are concerts of a chorus with no orchestra named among the performers were left out (judged by the title and the performer line of the page; the chorus was formerly called the Bucheon Philharmonic Chorus). Concerts such as recitals by chorus members, whose performer line names no ensemble, may remain. A subscription concert of the chorus in which the orchestra also played stays in the table but is not counted as a Bucheon Phil subscription concert. Where several dates share one program cell in the Busan record book (tours, for example), the program is entered for each of those dates; encore lines and lines for activities within a concert (instrument try-outs, storytelling, comic sketches) were not counted. A line naming a composer and an arranger counts for the composer; a line naming only an arranger has no composer.

Data: Suwon City Arts Group, yearly concert listings; Bucheon City Arts Group, concert archive; Busan Cultural Center, Busan Philharmonic record book 1962–2021 (read on 17 November 2026; only the facts of works and composers were tabulated, and the pages and the booklet themselves are not reproduced).

8. Limits#

  • The Vienna and Berlin Philharmonics and the Concertgebouw appear only through their Carnegie Hall concerts; their home seasons can be searched on their websites, but we found no downloadable public data.
  • Seoul attributions rest on titles and concert pages. Unattributed works are not random (section 6). The Seoul data list planned programs, including concerts later canceled or changed, with no cancellation flag; the New York records are of concerts actually given.
  • Carnegie Hall composers were matched by name, without Wikidata IDs. Namesakes were separated by a hand-made table.
  • Arrangements follow New York Philharmonic archive practice: simple arrangements count for the original composer, recompositions in the arranger's own catalog (Pulcinella and the like) for the arranger.
  • In section 7 the Suwon and Bucheon data are programs announced on website concert pages, and the Busan data come from the orchestra's own record book. For Suwon and Bucheon, concerts whose program exists only as an image, or whose program block is empty, are missing, and some lines of text could not be read by the rules; there is no reason to think what is missing is random. The sources differ in kind and in the years they cover, so the four-way tables use subscription concerts in the years all four cover. What an orchestra numbers as a subscription concert also differs.
  • Cosine and top-10 share measure only what is played and how often, not how well.

Data: New York Philharmonic Performance History (CC0); Carnegie Hall Data Lab (CC0); Hartmann-Enke, Gewandhaus concert programs (CC BY 4.0); Seoul Open Data Plaza OA-22909, OA-22910 (KOGL Type 1); Wikidata (CC0).
