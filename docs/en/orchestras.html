Orchestra repertoire compared

Repertoire of orchestras with public performance records, counted in the same unit and set side by side. No ranking.

1. Why there is no ranking

A composite score is only as good as its weights, and there is no outside standard to justify them. Public performance records are also concentrated in large US orchestras and the Seoul Philharmonic. The Vienna and Berlin Philharmonics and the Concertgebouw have searchable concert archives on their websites, but we found no public bulk download or API for the full record, so on this page they appear only through their Carnegie Hall concerts.

Critics' polls exist (Gramophone 2008, Bachtrack 2015 and 2023), but the Bachtrack polls reflect the views of 16 (2015) and 15 (2023) critics. This page sets out one comparison table per measure instead.

3. Can the Carnegie Hall records be trusted?

We measured the same orchestra from two sources. Composer shares for the New York Philharmonic were computed from its own archive and from the Carnegie Hall records, and compared by cosine similarity (1 means identical distributions). In the 1890s–1960s, covering the years Carnegie Hall was the orchestra's home (1892–1962), the two agree closely (0.92–0.99). After the move to Lincoln Center in 1962 its Carnegie appearances shrink to a few special concerts and the similarity falls to 0.24–0.64. Carnegie figures on this page therefore describe what an orchestra played at Carnegie Hall.

5. Leipzig and New York in the nineteenth century

The Gewandhaus programmes (1781–1895) and the New York Philharmonic records overlap from the 1840s to the 1890s and are compared decade by decade. The cosine between their composer shares falls from 0.92 in the 1850s to 0.67 in the 1890s (the Gewandhaus data end with the concert of 28 March 1895, so the 1890s cover 1890 to March 1895). These data cannot say why. The figure is sensitive to the name-matching table: in the first draft, before namesakes were separated, the 1840s value was 0.71.

6. Seoul and New York — subscription concerts 2009–2025

Both orchestras' subscription concerts are matched by concert date (2009–2025). New York seasons start in September, so its programmes are selected by first concert date rather than by season. Of 1,586 Seoul Philharmonic works, 1,362 (85.9%) have an attributed composer. The New York Philharmonic has 4,913 subscription work performances; 66 entries that do not name a single composer (traditional, anthems, unknown, non-works, and joint credits such as "Perlmutter / Wohl") are left out.

Seoul values are ranges because some works have unknown composers. The low end of the top-10 share assumes all 224 unattributed works fall outside the top ten; the high end assumes they all belong to the top ten. The bracketed value divides by attributed works only. For living composers, the unknowns are the 224 unattributed works plus 38 works by composers without known dates. The NY Phil top-10 value is the mean and 95% range of 500 draws of the same number of works (random seed 0).

  • The repertoire is very similar. Cosine 0.93.
  • Seoul leans more on its top composers. Seoul's low end, 39.7%, is above the top of the NY range, 38.6%, so the order holds however the unattributed works fall. The gap is 1.1 percentage points, so it is sensitive to attribution errors.
  • Cancelled and changed concerts do not change this. The Seoul data include concerts that were cancelled or whose programme changed. There are 63 subscription entries for 2020, against 37 to 52 in the other years from 2015 to 2025. Without 2020 and 2021, Seoul's low end is 40.8% and the top of the NY range 39.0%, so the gap widens.
  • No verdict on living composers. Seoul's range is wide and contains the NY value.

Sorted roughly by title, the 224 unattributed works are 74 bare genre titles such as "Violin Concerto", 33 marked as premieres or commissions, and 117 others, many of them by Korean composers or contemporary pieces whose composer we could not establish.

How composers were attached

Programme strings were split into works, then attributed in this order: a catalogue number (BWV, K., D. and so on); an exact match to a Carnegie Hall title; then reference titles sharing genre, number, opus and key, used only when they point to one composer. A title with its own distinctive words must share one with the reference titles, and a stated key must match. What the rules cannot settle goes to a hand-made table. Bare genre titles such as "Violin Concerto", and titles with more than one candidate composer, were left unattributed.

7. Limits

  • The Vienna and Berlin Philharmonics and the Concertgebouw appear only through their Carnegie Hall concerts; their home seasons can be searched on their websites, but we found no downloadable public data.
  • Seoul attributions rest on titles and concert pages. Unattributed works are not random (section 6). The Seoul data list planned programmes, including concerts later cancelled or changed, with no cancellation flag; the New York records are of concerts actually given.
  • Carnegie Hall composers were matched by name, without Wikidata IDs. Namesakes were separated by a hand-made table.
  • Arrangements follow New York Philharmonic archive practice: simple arrangements count for the original composer, recompositions in the arranger's own catalogue (Pulcinella and the like) for the arranger.
  • Cosine and top-10 share measure only what is played and how often, not how well.
