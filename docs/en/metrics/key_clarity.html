Key clarity

At a glance

What the data show
Among the collections, Mozart's piano sonatas have the highest median (0.91) and Bach's solo works (violin, cello, flute) the lowest (0.85). Within the piano sonatas: Beethoven 0.88, Mozart 0.91.
Watch for
One value from the pitch-class distribution of the whole movement, so it tends to be higher for pieces that stay in one key and lower for pieces that modulate often or move chromatically; which factor contributes how much cannot be told from this value alone.

How closely the movement's pitch distribution resembles the typical distribution of a single key (new in 1.9).

Formula
Pearson correlation between the duration-weighted distribution of the 12 pitch classes and the Krumhansl–Kessler (1982) major and minor key profiles, rotated through all 24 keys; the largest value is used (Krumhansl–Schmuckler key finding). The annotated key is not used. Grace notes are left out, and a tied continuation is merged into the note it continues and counted once. A continuation whose preceding note cannot be found is not counted as a new note (it remains a sounding note only for the top-line metrics).
How to read it
The closer to 1, the more the distribution looks like that of one key. Across the site's 20 collections the collection medians run from 0.85 (Bach's solo works) to 0.91 (Mozart's sonatas); the median over all movements is 0.875.
Caveats
One value from the pitch-class distribution of the whole movement, so it tends to be higher for pieces that stay in one key and lower for pieces that modulate often or move chromatically; which factor contributes how much cannot be told from this value alone. Short pieces have few notes, so the value is less stable. The profiles come from listening experiments and may fit some periods and styles better than others.
