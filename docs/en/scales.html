Scales and tuning

How the octave (1,200 cents) has been divided, compared across periods and regions. The cent is a unit for the distance in pitch between two notes (an interval). It measures the ratio of their frequencies on a logarithmic scale, so the same interval has the same size in cents at any pitch: one piano semitone is 100 cents, and the octave, a doubling of frequency, is 1,200. Each dot is a note; the gray ticks are piano equal temperament (the octave split into twelve equal semitones, 100 cents apart). The buttons play the scale, synthesized by your browser on the spot.

Before you start: setting notes by ratio#

This section gathers the arithmetic behind the rulers below: how an interval is written as a ratio of frequencies, what changes when the ratio is one of small whole numbers, how Pythagorean tuning, sanfen sunyi, just intonation and equal temperament each fix their notes, and how the difference shows in enharmonic pairs. Every value is computed from a definition, not measured. The buttons sound above middle C (261.63 Hz).

Frequency ratios and cents

Pitch is measured by how many times a second a sound vibrates (frequency, in hertz, Hz: vibrations per second). The interval between two notes is fixed by the ratio of their frequencies, not the difference (f₁ and f₂ below are the two frequencies). From middle C (the C near the middle of the piano keyboard, C4: 261.63 Hz) to the C an octave up (523.26 Hz) the difference is 261.63 Hz, and to the next C (1046.52 Hz) it is 523.26 Hz, twice as much; the ratio is 2:1 both times. The cent turns the ratio into a logarithm: log₂ x is the power to which 2 must be raised to give x (log₂ 2 = 1, log₂ 4 = 2).

cents = 1200 × log₂(f₂ ÷ f₁)
e.g. 3:2 → 1200 × log₂ 1.5 = 1200 × 0.58496 = 701.96 cents
ratio 2 → 1,200 cents
ratio 2^(1/12) = 1.05946 → 100 cents
Ratios multiply, cents add. A perfect fifth (3/2) with a perfect fourth (4/3) on top makes an octave: 3/2 × 4/3 = 2 → 701.96 + 498.04 = 1,200

An equal-tempered semitone multiplies the frequency by 2^(1/12) = 1.0595. On the ratio ruler above, the semitones therefore widen toward the right (the first, 1 → 1.0595, spans 0.0595; the last, 1.8877 → 2, spans 0.1123), while on the cent ruler below each is 100 cents. Cents turn multiplying ratios into adding. The colored dots and dashed lines are the twelve main just ratios of the table below. On the cent ruler, the horizontal distance from a colored dot to the end of the nearest gray line is its difference from equal temperament.

Current order: by size (semitones, then cents).

When two tones of slightly different frequency sound together, the sound swells and fades as many times a second as their frequencies differ: these are beats. In an interval of ratio p/q (p the numerator, q the denominator) the p-th harmonic of the lower tone and the q-th harmonic of the upper tone fall at the same height (harmonics are the next subsection). Tuned just, these two harmonics coincide and do not beat against each other (other harmonics that lie close may still leave some roughness); tuned equal, they miss slightly and beat as often as the table says. In an equal-tempered major third above middle C, the lower tone's 5th harmonic (5 × 261.63 = 1308.2 Hz) and the upper tone's 4th (4 × 329.63 = 1318.5 Hz) beat 10.4 times a second; in an equal-tempered fifth, 0.9 times. Assuming a quality of tone whose 2nd and 3rd partials are strong, 4th and 5th moderate and higher ones rapidly weaker, Helmholtz called consonances those ratios at which two tones sounded together form no beats between their partials, or only very weak ones (On the Sensations of Tone, tr. Ellis, 3rd ed., 1895, ch. X, p. 194). Values in parentheses are beats between harmonics above the 8th (p greater than 8 in the ratio p/q); they exist in the arithmetic but cannot be heard with these buttons, whose tone stops at the 8th harmonic.

Harmonics

A note from a string or a pipe is usually not one pure tone (a sound with a single frequency and no harmonics) of frequency f but can be written as pure tones at f, 2f, 3f, 4f … sounding together. Each component is a harmonic, and the row of them is the harmonic series. In the ideal case a uniform flexible string fixed at both ends and a uniform cylindrical pipe open at both ends can vibrate at every whole multiple, and a uniform cylindrical pipe closed at one end at odd multiples only (f, 3f, 5f …); not every possible mode actually sounds. Real instruments depart a little from these values because of their material and shape, and their harmonics differ in strength. Measured harmonics of real instruments are not on this page yet.

Each dot is a harmonic (n above it). Harmonics 2, 4, 8 and 16 are octaves of the fundamental; the 3rd lies an octave and a fifth up (1902.0 cents), the 5th two octaves and a major third up (2786.3). The intervals between neighboring harmonics are 2:1, 3:2, 4:3, 5:4, 6:5: the small-number intervals of the table above appear in order at the start of the series. The 7th harmonic (3368.8 cents) lies 31.2 cents below the nearest equal-tempered note, and the 11th is 48.7 cents off, nearly halfway between two. Hover over a dot for its values.

The other buttons on this page use a triangle wave, which has odd harmonics only, the n-th at amplitude 1/n². The beats of the major third (5:4) come from the upper tone's 4th harmonic, which a triangle wave lacks, so the major-third beats in the table above do not arise in a triangle wave (some faint roughness between other odd harmonics may remain). The buttons in this section that compare two or more notes together therefore use harmonics 1-8 at amplitude 1/n (the buttons above that play harmonics one by one use pure tones).

Four ways of computing the notes

Pythagorean tuning — stack pure fifths (3:2) only, and divide by octaves (2) to bring each into the range 1 to 2.

note = (3/2)ᵏ ÷ 2ᵐ (k fifths stacked, m octaves taken off)
e.g. E = (3/2)⁴ ÷ 2² = 81/16 ÷ 4 = 81/64 → 407.8 cents
F = (3/2)⁻¹ × 2 = 4/3
12 fifths = (3/2)¹² = 129.746, 7 octaves = 2⁷ = 128 → their ratio 531441/524288 = 1.01364 → 23.46 cents (the Pythagorean comma). A positive power of 3 is odd and a positive power of 2 even, so they can never be equal: no number of stacked fifths ever matches a whole number of octaves.

Sanfen sunyi (三分損益) — divide the length of a pitch pipe (the pipe that sounds a lü's reference note) into three and take away one part (sun, 損) or add one (yi, 益) to get the next pipe. The Shiji 'Lüshu' starts from 81.

律數：九九八十一以爲宮。三分去一，五十四以爲徴。三分益一，七十二以爲商。三分去一，四十八以爲羽。三分益一，六十四以爲角。

(Translation) The lü numbers: nine times nine, 81, is taken as gong. Divide into three and take away one: 54 is zhi. Divide into three and add one: 72 is shang. Take away one: 48 is yu. Add one: 64 is jue.

Sima Qian, Shiji 25, 'Lüshu' (zh.wikisource revision 2687752)

Read as pitch, the text's "begets upward" (shangsheng) and "begets downward" (xiasheng) run against the arithmetic: shangsheng (yi) lengthens the pipe and lowers the note by a fourth, xiasheng (sun) shortens it and raises the note by a fifth. In this arithmetic the seven pipes the text calls upper are all longer than the five it calls lower. What the text meant by upper and lower cannot be settled from the text alone; the page only notes that the arithmetic matches pipe length. Pipe length is taken as inversely proportional to frequency (ideal uniform cylindrical pipes of the same kind and conditions, without the end correction). Sun is then a fifth up and yi a fourth down, so sanfen sunyi gives the same values as Pythagorean tuning (linzhong 702.0, taicou 203.9, …). Sun and yi do not always alternate. The text puts dalü among the upper pipes, so ruibin begets dalü by shangsheng, that is by yi; yi thus comes twice in a row (yingzhong to ruibin, ruibin to dalü), and every one of the twelve pipes stays between huangzhong (81) and half of it (40.5) (sun at ruibin would give 37.9, outside that range). By length the seven 'upper' pipes are 56.9 or longer and the five 'lower' ones 54.0 or shorter. One more sun after zhonglü gives a length of 39.955, shorter than half of huangzhong (40.5), so 23.46 cents above huangzhong an octave up: by the Pythagorean comma the twelve pipes fail to close. The same is why the twelfth pipe, zhonglü, lies at 521.5 cents rather than at 4/3 (498.0 cents).

Enharmonic notes

C♯ and D♭ share a key on the piano, and in equal temperament both lie 100 cents above middle C. There the two names give one sound. In the two ratio calculations below, computing the two notes separately gives different values.

Pythagorean: C♯ = 7 fifths up = 3⁷/2¹¹ = 2187/2048 → 113.7 cents
D♭ = 5 fifths down = 2⁸/3⁵ = 256/243 → 90.2 cents
C♯ is 23.5 cents above D♭ (their ratio 3¹²/2¹⁹, the Pythagorean comma)
Just (one example): C♯ = a major third above A (5/3) = 5/3 × 5/4 ÷ 2 = 25/24 → 70.7 cents
D♭ = a major third below F (4/3) = 4/3 ÷ 5/4 = 16/15 → 111.7 cents
here C♯ is 41.1 cents below D♭ (their ratio D♭ ÷ C♯ = 16/15 ÷ 25/24 = 128/125)
Equal temperament: C♯ = D♭ = 2^(1/12) → 100 cents

In just intonation C♯ and D♭ depend on the note and the interval they are built from, so neither has a single value; the line above is one example. The twelve pipes of sanfen sunyi have one value per place. With huangzhong on C, dalü takes that place, and since it comes seven fifths up it has the C♯ value (113.7 cents). The list of twelve ends at zhonglü, so the D♭ value never appears.

Together, the Pythagorean pair (about 23 cents apart) and the just pair (about 41 cents apart) beat quickly; the equal-tempered pair is one note and does not beat.

1. Western tuning — values defined by ratios#

All nine rows are theoretical values defined by ratios, not measurements of instruments. Just intonation tunes intervals to small whole-number frequency ratios such as 3:2 (the fifth) and 5:4 (the major third); Pythagorean tuning stacks only pure fifths (3:2); quarter-comma meantone narrows the fifths slightly so that the major thirds come out pure (5:4); a comma is a small difference between two intervals. Closed into twelve notes on a keyboard, it leaves a few major thirds and one fifth far out of tune (that fifth is called the wolf fifth). Equal temperament splits the octave (2:1) into twelve equal semitones, so within one octave every interval other than the unison and the octave has a frequency ratio that is not a whole-number ratio. In the bottom row, 12-tone equal temperament, every dot sits on a gray tick; in the other rows the distance of a dot from the gray tick is its difference from equal temperament. The major third above the first note is 400 cents in equal temperament, 408 cents in Pythagorean tuning and 386 cents in just intonation and quarter-comma meantone (100 cents is one piano semitone). Sanfen sunyi (三分損益) shortens or lengthens a pipe by a third, which amounts to stacking pure fifths. The twelfth note, 仲呂, lands at 521.5 cents rather than 4/3 (498 cents), because twelve fifths overshoot the octave by the Pythagorean comma (23.5 cents). The twelve yul of Korean court music follow the same series; in the ratio table of Yoo et al. (2011), jungnyeo (仲呂) is 1.35. The note sets of pyeongjo and gyemyeonjo follow convention, and whether performers actually placed jungnyeo near 521 cents cannot be told from these values. The texts of the Shiji and the Lüshi chunqiu, and the lengths of the twelve pipes, are in 'Before you start'.

The names in (陰旋) and yō (陽旋) for Japan's two five-note scales come from Uehara Rokushirō (Zokugaku senritsu kō, 1895, p. 79). He separated the scale of urban music such as zokusō, nagauta and jiuta (miyako-bushi) from that of rural songs such as boat, packhorse and rice-planting songs (inaka-bushi), and called the first in and the second yō. In both, the fifth note differs going up and coming down. He measured the in scale by string lengths on a monochord (pp. 90–91) and gave both scales as frequency ratios on pp. 104–107. For the ascending fifth note of the in scale he gave 12/7, 7/4 or 9/5, depending on the school and the shamisen tuning; 7/4 is used here. The table on pp. 105–106 prints 15/16 and 4/8 for the second and third notes; they are read as 16/15 and 4/3 to match the table's own interval column and the string-length ratios on p. 91. Of the yō ratios Uehara wrote that they came from only a few trials and could not be called exact. 'Up and down' switches to the hollow-dot note on the way down.

For the koto's hira-jōshi tuning, Ellis (1885) printed the tunings of two players he measured with Hipkins at the Japanese Village in Knightsbridge, London, beside the values in a report sent by Isawa Shūji (p. 522). He noted that the woman's fifth was sharp and her master's flat, 36 cents apart. Between strings IV and V the report has 386 cents; the two players tuned 362 and 346.

Two gayageum studies supply measured values for a Korean instrument. Ko (2021) measured with Praat the gayageum pitches of Gyeonggi folk songs played by Lee Ilseon, Kim Jukpa and Seong Geumyeon on records from 1928 to 1960, and gave each string's deviation from equal temperament with the mean of string 6 (Do) set to zero (p. 183, Table 102). In Lee Ilseon's record string 11 (upper Do) lies 1,141 cents above string 6, 59 cents short of an octave. Yang (2013) measured, in jinyangjo recordings of gayageum sanjo by Kim Byeongho, Kim Jukpa, Seong Geumyeon and Ham Dongjeongwol, cheong, the central note of gyemyeonjo, and the two notes of the bent figure (kkeongneun eum). The pitch of hwangjong changed over time

A scale is a set of ratios; the actual pitch is fixed by the reference note hwangjong (黃鍾). A study of 241 surviving stone-chime slabs (pyeongyeong and teukgyeong) in Korean collections grouped the stones by the cyclical year or the re-tuning date inscribed on them and estimated hwangjong from each group. A cyclical year recurs every 60 years, so the jeongyu and gapja groups do not fix a single year of manufacture.

The 24 quarter tones of Arabic music were set out by Mīkhāʾīl Mushāqa of Damascus in the 19th century. He counted 24 quarters to the octave and divided the seven steps from rast into 4, 3, 3, 4, 4, 3 and 3 quarters (tr. Eli Smith 1847, p. 176). He gave a construction that shortens the quarters along the string in a fixed ratio so that they come out as equal intervals (pp. 213–215). They are drawn here as 50 cents each, which puts the third degree at 350 cents, halfway between a major and a minor third. Yet after comparing two tamburs Mushāqa also wrote that the Arab division into quarters was in error and the Greek division right (p. 216). The 1932 Cairo Congress of Arab Music set up a committee to fix the scale, but it reached no final agreed proposal (Bozkurt 2025). From the pitch data of the Congress recordings (ORD-CC32), the editor averaged the per-recording histograms and recomputed the peaks. In 13 Egyptian recordings of Rast the third degree lies at 355 cents; in 3 Iraqi recordings, at 376. In 17 Egyptian recordings of Bayati the second degree lies at 150 cents. The histogram bins are 20 cents wide, so differences of a few cents should not be read into. There are also values measured on an instrument. In 1937 Mokhtar and Mosharrafa of the Egyptian University in Cairo had three tuners appointed by the Royal Institute of Oriental Music tune a qanun (strings stretched over a sounding board) to the principal modes, measured the frequency of each string and averaged the three tuners. They used a valve oscillator (an electrical source of a tone at a chosen pitch) calibrated against tuning forks, and the method of beats (two tones of slightly different frequency sounded together swell and fade periodically), and gave the experimental error as about 0.1 per cent (about 2 cents) (Nature 140: 548–549). The octave came out divided into 14 note positions at unequal distances (the last dot on the ruler is the octave note). The authors give four principal modes by note number only, without names. The first starts on the note called Rast and agrees, note by note to within 6.3 cents, with the rast of Mushāqa drawn above (quarter tones as 50 cents: 0, 200, 350, 500, 700, 900, 1,050). Its third degree is at 356 cents and its seventh at 1,047. The authors single out these neutral thirds and neutral sevenths (intervals between the major and the minor) as the most characteristic feature of the scheme. The same table gives values from the 1932 Congress for seven of the notes; they differ from the values of the authors by 0 to 9.1 cents. Values for each tuner are not given, so how far the three tuners differed cannot be told. This is a qanun tuned in advance, and the pitches actually sung or played on the oud (the Arab lute) may differ.

The 22 śrutis come from Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra: the seven notes of the ṣaḍja-grāma take 4, 3, 2, 4, 4, 3 and 2 śrutis (28.24–26; tr. Ghosh, vol. II, p. 8). The text gives the number of śrutis, not their size, so they are drawn here as equal. According to Ramanathan (1981), for Bharata the śruti was the least perceptible difference in pitch between two sounds, while for Dattila and Śārṅgadeva it was first the pitch position so distinguished (p. 33). It is a unit for measuring the size of svaras and sorting them into the two grāmas; formulated for classification, it 'had no existence in performance' (p. 32). The śrutis within a svara are 'roughly equal' and their exact size is of no importance; the total of 22 has no sanctity, what matters being the number of śrutis in each svara (pp. 34–35). Expressing the position of a particular svara of the modern classification as a ratio such as 9/8 or 10/9 only puts known facts in a different language (pp. 36–37). The 22 śrutis tested in the measurement study below are not this drawing but a modern ratio system that divides the octave into 22 unequal steps. Measuring present-day singing, Serrà et al. (2011) compared interval histograms of 233 Carnatic and 133 Hindustani recordings with just intonation and equal temperament. Carnatic singing was closer to just intonation (correlation 0.552 vs 0.448), Hindustani closer to equal temperament (0.723 vs 0.641). At the seven positions found only in that 22-śruti system, Hindustani showed no peaks and Carnatic only small contributions at three. The authors concluded that the 22 śrutis lack strong quantitative support but cannot be ruled out.

Bel's Toḍī values are note positions averaged over the stable parts of the melodic line only: windows of 0.1, 0.4 and 0.2 seconds removed irregular curve segments, segments leaving an 80-cent band, and segments with a steep slope (p. 14). Bel wrote that fa♯ is very hard to locate, being barely suggested in the melodic movement (p. 15). That the notes of a rāga are not fixed points also shows in measurements of Carnatic singing. Measuring the recordings of nineteen singers, Viraraghavan et al. (2018) found that the precision of notes held at one pitch improves with duration and settles at about 10 cents beyond 0.5 seconds, while the points where the pitch curve turns within a gamaka (ornamental movement) have errors with a standard deviation of about 60 cents. These are per-note errors measured in Carnatic music, so they cannot be compared directly with the spread between performers in the Hindustani Toḍī row. That one svara is sung differently in different rāgas was shown by Koduri et al. (2014), who drew the pitch distribution of each svara in 28 varṇams in seven rāgas sung by five young professional singers with only the drone. The Chopi timbila of Mozambique divides the octave into seven nearly equal steps. Over his fieldwork Tracey measured several hundred instruments with 54 tuning forks spanning 212–424 vibrations in steps of four, and wrote that he could fix a pitch to within two vibrations (Chopi Musicians, 1948, p. 122). He visited Chopi villages every year from 1940 and had noted most of the scales in the book before a meeting at Quissico on 26 September 1943, where instrument makers and orchestra leaders said that, whatever their differences over the pitch of the tonic Hombe, each was aiming at 'an even scale of seven intervals, all alike' (pp. 123–124). In five orchestras of the Zavala District the steps range from 141 to 207 cents (171 for seven equal steps), and Hombe lay at 252–276 vibrations (p. 125, Diagram IV). The pale green bars on the five-orchestra mean show the spread between orchestras. The single-instrument row gives the values Tracey printed in 1958, the same as the Zavala orchestra in the 1948 diagram. In the 'Mozambique' article of the New Grove, second edition (2001), Andrew Tracey calls the tuning equiheptatonic, each step about 170 cents, and says the absolute pitch of the tonic does not vary from village to village by more than about 100 cents; the Hombe of the five orchestras in the 1948 diagram (252–276 vibrations) spans about 157 cents, but a note under the diagram's title says that Zandamela, the highest (276), has since retuned to Zavala's Hombe of 252; the other four (252–260 vibrations) span about 54 cents.

4. Modes — the same seven notes, a different start#

A mode is a scale framework defined by which notes are used and which of them is the final, the note on which a melody in the mode usually comes to rest. Today's major scale has the same arrangement as the Ionian mode, the natural minor as the Aeolian. Here modes are told apart by the order of tones and semitones within an octave, and the same seven notes are laid out an octave from different starting notes for comparison (some theories divide modes further by final and range; see 'Where the names came from' below). The seven white keys of a piano alone give different arrangements: change the starting key and the tones and semitones fall in different places. Between white keys there are only two semitones, E–F and B–C (where there is no black key); each time the starting key moves up one, the two semitones move one step earlier in the octave, and a semitone in first place wraps round to last (from E to F). The names are today's.

The 'Tuning' buttons play these seven rows in 12-tone equal temperament, Pythagorean tuning (stacked pure fifths) or quarter-comma meantone. Only the pitches of the white keys change; the order of tones and semitones does not. The major third C–E is 400 cents in equal temperament, 408 in Pythagorean tuning and 386 in meantone. Middle C (261.63 Hz) is C, and each mode starts at the pitch of its own starting key.

Where the names came from

Today's names and the ancient Greek names are the same words for different arrangements. Cleonides' Harmonic Introduction, of uncertain date, says the octave has seven species and that the ancients called them Mixolydian, Lydian, Phrygian, Dorian, Hypolydian and Hypophrygian, the seventh 'common or Locrian or Hypodorian' (sections 9–10, tr. Strunk). He bounds each species by Greek note names and says where the semitones fall, counted from the bottom and the top, in the diatonic genus. Matched to the white keys by those semitones, the Greek Dorian runs from E to E, which is today's Phrygian. In the same way the Greek Phrygian starts on D (today's Dorian), the Lydian on C (today's Ionian) and the Mixolydian on B (today's Locrian). The match is only in the order of tones and semitones; it does not mean that the pitches or tunings of Greek music were those of the white keys.

Lines of one color join the same name. Each row is the white key on which the octave arrangement of the species or mode starts (for Glarean's authentic modes this note is the final).

The title page of Glarean's Dodecachordon (Basel, 1547) lists six authentic modes with their finals: Dorius D, Phrygius E, Lydius F, Mixolydius G, Aeolius A and Ionicus C. The same table names, apart, a Hyperaeolius on B and its plagal, a Hyperphrygius (the F on the plagal side is the bottom of its range); Glarean set these two aside and built twelve modes, six authentic and six plagal (book I, ch. 21; German tr. p. 49). He wrote that his contemporaries had counted 'eight modes or tones', and that by nature there are only seven modes, as there are only seven species of the octave, but twelve if they are divided into authentic and plagal (book II, ch. 5; German tr. p. 65, a misprint for p. 56). On the same page he wrote that changing a single semitone can alter a mode's character entirely, and that musicians of his day, by inverting the old sixth and seventh modes, had made two Ionian modes, the new fifth and sixth, now more used than the Lydian (the numbers are Glarean's old and new numbering). Set on the white keys, the two sources attach four names (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian) to different arrangements. Aeolian and Ionian were attached to A and C in Glarean's table, and 'Locrian', today the name of the mode on B, was for Cleonides another name for the species starting on A.
