Scales and tuning

How the octave (1,200 cents) has been divided, compared across periods and regions. Each dot is a note; the grey ticks are piano equal temperament (100 cents apart). The buttons play the scale, synthesised by your browser on the spot.

Every scale is played on the same reference, middle C (261.63 Hz); the instruments themselves sit at other pitches. 'Against equal temperament' follows each note with the nearest equal-tempered note. The sound is a synthesised triangle wave, not the instrument's timbre. 'Theory' marks values set by ratios or rules; 'measured' marks values taken from instruments or performances. A hollow dot is a note used only when descending; a dashed ring marks an octave (1,200 cents) that the source does not give and that is added only for playback.

2. East Asia — sanfen sunyi (三分損益) and Japan's two scales

Sanfen sunyi (三分損益) shortens and lengthens a pipe by a third in turn, which amounts to stacking pure fifths. The twelfth note, 仲呂, lands at 521.5 cents rather than 4/3 (498 cents), because twelve fifths overshoot the octave by the Pythagorean comma (23.5 cents). The twelve yul of Korean court music follow the same series; in the ratio table of Yoo et al. (2011), jungnyeo (仲呂) is 1.35. The note sets of pyeongjo and gyemyeonjo follow convention, and whether performers actually placed jungnyeo near 521 cents cannot be told from these values.

The names in (陰旋) and yō (陽旋) for Japan's two five-note scales come from Uehara Rokushirō (Zokugaku senritsu kō, 1895, p. 79). He separated the scale of urban music such as zokusō, nagauta and jiuta (miyako-bushi) from that of rural songs such as boat, packhorse and rice-planting songs (inaka-bushi), and called the first in and the second yō. In both, the fifth note differs going up and coming down. He measured the in scale by string lengths on a monochord (pp. 90–91) and gave both scales as frequency ratios on pp. 104–107. For the ascending fifth note of the in scale he gave 12/7, 7/4 or 9/5, depending on the school and the shamisen tuning; 7/4 is used here. The table on pp. 105–106 prints 15/16 and 4/8 for the second and third notes; they are read as 16/15 and 4/3 to match the table's own interval column and the string-length ratios on p. 91. Of the yō ratios Uehara wrote that they came from only a few trials and could not be called exact. 'Up and down' switches to the hollow-dot note on the way down.

For the koto's hira-jōshi tuning, Ellis (1885) printed the tunings of two players he measured with Hipkins at the Japanese Village in Knightsbridge, London, beside the values in a report sent by Isawa Shūji (p. 522). He noted that the woman's fifth was sharp and her master's flat, 36 cents apart. Between strings IV and V the report has 386 cents; the two players tuned 362 and 346.

Two gayageum studies supply measured values for a Korean instrument. Ko (2021) measured with Praat the gayageum pitches of Gyeonggi folk songs played by Lee Ilseon, Kim Jukpa and Seong Geumyeon on records from 1928 to 1960, and gave each string's deviation from equal temperament with the mean of string 6 (Do) set to zero (p. 183, Table 102). In all four records Re is 51–69 cents below equal temperament and La at least 30 cents below, and the Re–Mi step is wider than a major second. In Lee Ilseon's record string 11 (upper Do) lies 1,141 cents above string 6, 59 cents short of an octave. Yang (2013) measured, in jinyangjo recordings of gayageum sanjo by Kim Byeongho, Kim Jukpa, Seong Geumyeon and Ham Dongjeongwol, cheong, the central note of gyemyeonjo, and the two notes of the bent figure (kkeongneun eum). The values shown are for the middle register, which has the most samples (pp. 84, 87–88). From cheong to the lower bent note is 68–111 cents, far narrower than the major second (200 cents) used in transcription; to the upper bent note, 350–354 cents, wider than a minor third (300). Ham Dongjeongwol's upper note follows the cent value printed in the table (353.8); the table's own frequencies give 349.7 cents. Cheong's octave measured 1,193–1,206 cents (p. 47). Ko measured four records by three players (two songs by Kim Jukpa), Yang one recording each by four players. The values do not stand for a player or school as a whole. For a wind instrument, Unjung Nam (1998) measured about ten stable (non-vibrato) samples per tone in three solo p'iri recordings by Chung Chaekook (two Sangnyeongsan and Sujecheon) and drew the average intervals between tones (p. 244, Fig. 1). In the Sangnyeongsan of Pyeongjo hoesang hwang–tae is 141 cents; in Sujecheon tae–go is 204. The five printed intervals of each work, however, sum to 1,208, 1,288 and 1,177 cents rather than an octave, and the article does not say why, so only the five tones are drawn and the octave is the dashed playback ring. Nam notes that the wide pitch bends of the p'iri keep these averages from settling the scale on their own.

Lim (2013) measured, in the jinyangjo and jungmori of the Kim Jukpa, Kim Byeongho and Kang Taehong schools of gayageum sanjo, the interval between jing (c′) and ttang (d′) in each mode (p. 114, Table 140). Staff notation writes it as a major second (200 cents); all fourteen values are narrower, and all but Kang Taehong's gyeongdeureum (86 cents) lie between a minor and a major second. It is one interval rather than a scale, so it is shown as a table rather than on the ruler.

The pitch of hwangjong changed over time

A scale is a set of ratios; the actual pitch is fixed by the reference note hwangjong (黃鍾). A study of 241 surviving stone-chime slabs (pyeongyeong and teukgyeong) in Korean collections grouped the stones by the cyclical year or the re-tuning date inscribed on them and estimated hwangjong from each group. A cyclical year recurs every 60 years, so the jeongyu and gapja groups do not fix a single year of manufacture.

3. Theory and measurement

The notes a theory prescribes and the notes actually tuned or sung differ. Theory and measurement for the same scale sit side by side. The gamelan rows average dozens of sets; the orange bars show ±1 standard deviation (population SD over the sets included). In the gamelan and ranat ek means shown here the octave is wider than 1,200 cents (1,214–1,217). Where the source gives no octave note, 1,200 cents is added for playback.

The 24 quarter tones of Arabic music were set out by Mīkhāʾīl Mushāqa of Damascus in the 19th century. He counted 24 quarters to the octave and divided the seven steps from rast into 4, 3, 3, 4, 4, 3 and 3 quarters (tr. Eli Smith 1847, p. 176). He gave a construction that shortens the quarters along the string in a fixed ratio so that they come out as equal intervals (pp. 213–215). They are drawn here as 50 cents each, which puts the third degree at 350 cents, halfway between a major and a minor third. Yet after comparing two tamburs Mushāqa also wrote that the Arab division into quarters was in error and the Greek division right (p. 216). The 1932 Cairo Congress of Arab Music set up a committee to fix the scale, but it reached no final agreed proposal (Bozkurt 2025). From the pitch data of the Congress recordings (ORD-CC32), the editor averaged the per-recording histograms and recomputed the peaks. In 13 Egyptian recordings of Rast the third degree lies at 355 cents; in 3 Iraqi recordings, at 376. In 17 Egyptian recordings of Bayati the second degree lies at 150 cents. The histogram bins are 20 cents wide, so differences of a few cents should not be read into.

The 22 śrutis come from Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra: the seven notes of the ṣaḍja-grāma take 4, 3, 2, 4, 4, 3 and 2 śrutis (28.24–26; tr. Ghosh, vol. II, p. 8). The text gives the number of śrutis, not their size, so they are drawn here as equal. According to Ramanathan (1981), for Bharata the śruti was the least perceptible difference in pitch between two sounds, while for Dattila and Śārṅgadeva it was first the pitch position so distinguished (p. 33). It is a unit for measuring the size of svaras and sorting them into the two grāmas; formulated for classification, it 'had no existence in performance' (p. 32). The śrutis within a svara are 'roughly equal' and their exact size is of no importance; the total of 22 has no sanctity, what matters being the number of śrutis in each svara (pp. 34–35). Expressing the position of a particular svara of the modern classification as a ratio such as 9/8 or 10/9 only puts known facts in a different language (pp. 36–37). The 22 śrutis tested in the measurement study below are not this drawing but a modern ratio system that divides the octave into 22 unequal steps. Measuring present-day singing, Serrà et al. (2011) compared interval histograms of 233 Carnatic and 133 Hindustani recordings with just intonation and equal temperament. Carnatic singing was closer to just intonation (correlation 0.552 vs 0.448), Hindustani closer to equal temperament (0.723 vs 0.641). At the seven positions found only in that 22-śruti system, Hindustani showed no peaks and Carnatic only small contributions at three. The authors concluded that the 22 śrutis lack strong quantitative support but cannot be ruled out.

The Chopi timbila of Mozambique divides the octave into seven nearly equal steps. Over his fieldwork Tracey measured several hundred instruments with 54 tuning forks spanning 212–424 vibrations in steps of four, and wrote that he could fix a pitch to within two vibrations (Chopi Musicians, 1948, p. 122). He visited Chopi villages every year from 1940 and had noted most of the scales in the book before a meeting at Quissico on 26 September 1943, where instrument makers and orchestra leaders said that, whatever their differences over the pitch of the tonic Hombe, each was aiming at 'an even scale of seven intervals, all alike' (pp. 123–124). In five orchestras of the Zavala District the steps range from 141 to 207 cents (171 for seven equal steps), and Hombe lay at 252–276 vibrations (p. 125, Diagram IV). The orange bars on the five-orchestra mean show the spread between orchestras. The single-instrument row gives the values Tracey printed in 1958, the same as the Zavala orchestra in the 1948 diagram. In the 'Mozambique' article of the New Grove, second edition (2001), Andrew Tracey calls the tuning equiheptatonic, each step about 170 cents, and says the absolute pitch of the tonic does not vary from village to village by more than about 100 cents; the Hombe of the five orchestras in the 1948 diagram (252–276 vibrations) spans about 157 cents, but a note under the diagram's title says that Zandamela, the highest (276), has since retuned to Zavala's Hombe of 252; the other four (252–260 vibrations) span about 54 cents.

4. Modes — the same seven notes, a different start

Here modes are told apart by the order of tones and semitones within an octave, and the same seven notes are laid out an octave from different starting notes for comparison (some theories divide modes further by final and range; see 'Where the names came from' below). The seven white keys of a piano alone give different arrangements: change the starting key and the tones and semitones fall in different places. Between white keys there are only two semitones, E–F and B–C (where there is no black key); each time the starting key moves up one, the two semitones move one step earlier in the octave, and a semitone in first place wraps round to last (from E to F). The names are today's.

The 'Tuning' buttons play these seven rows in 12-tone equal temperament, Pythagorean tuning (stacked pure fifths) or quarter-comma meantone. Only the pitches of the white keys change; the order of tones and semitones does not. The major third C–E is 400 cents in equal temperament, 408 in Pythagorean tuning and 386 in meantone. Middle C (261.63 Hz) is C, and each mode starts at the pitch of its own starting key.

Where the names came from

Today's names and the ancient Greek names are the same words for different arrangements. Cleonides' Harmonic Introduction, of uncertain date, says the octave has seven species and that the ancients called them Mixolydian, Lydian, Phrygian, Dorian, Hypolydian and Hypophrygian, the seventh 'common or Locrian or Hypodorian' (sections 9–10, tr. Strunk). He bounds each species by Greek note names and says where the semitones fall, counted from the bottom and the top, in the diatonic genus. Matched to the white keys by those semitones, the Greek Dorian runs from E to E, which is today's Phrygian. In the same way the Greek Phrygian starts on D (today's Dorian), the Lydian on C (today's Ionian) and the Mixolydian on B (today's Locrian). The match is only in the order of tones and semitones; it does not mean that the pitches or tunings of Greek music were those of the white keys.

Lines of one color join the same name. Each row is the white key on which the octave arrangement of the species or mode starts (for Glarean's authentic modes this note is the final).

The title page of Glarean's Dodecachordon (Basel, 1547) lists six authentic modes with their finals: Dorius D, Phrygius E, Lydius F, Mixolydius G, Aeolius A and Ionicus C. The same table names, apart, a Hyperaeolius on B and its plagal, a Hyperphrygius (the F on the plagal side is the bottom of its range); Glarean set these two aside and built twelve modes, six authentic and six plagal (book I, ch. 21; German tr. p. 49). He wrote that his contemporaries had counted 'eight modes or tones', and that by nature there are only seven modes, as there are only seven species of the octave, but twelve if they are divided into authentic and plagal (book II, ch. 5; German tr. p. 65, a misprint for p. 56). On the same page he wrote that changing a single semitone can alter a mode's character entirely, and that musicians of his day, by inverting the old sixth and seventh modes, had made two Ionian modes, the new fifth and sixth, now more used than the Lydian (the numbers are Glarean's old and new numbering). Set on the white keys, the two sources attach four names (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian) to different arrangements. Aeolian and Ionian were attached to A and C in Glarean's table, and 'Locrian', today the name of the mode on B, was for Cleonides another name for the species starting on A.
