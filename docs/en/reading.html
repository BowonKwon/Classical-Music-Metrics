Reading edition

Baseball reads its players through batting average and OPS. Classical music can do the same, as far as scores and recordings are open as data. This is the prose edition of the site's main results; definitions and full tables live under Metrics.

1. Scores — 518 Bach movements, 161 Beethoven movements

From open annotated corpora: Bach's chorales, English and French Suites and solo works; Beethoven's complete string quartets and piano sonatas — 638,550 notes, measured by one codebase. Medians by collection:

Three things stand out. First, the chromatic ratio more than doubles from the chorales (0.06) to Beethoven's sonatas (0.15), yet Bach's unaccompanied solo works (0.11) and Beethoven's quartets (0.13) sit close together — the gap between composers can be smaller than the gap between forces, so this table alone cannot establish the cliché "Baroque diatonic, Classical chromatic" (it is not a genre-controlled comparison). Second, harmonic rhythm runs the other way: Bach's suites change chord about three times a bar, Beethoven fewer than twice. Third, the top-line leap ratio is lowest in chorale sopranos (0.14) and highest in Beethoven's sonatas (0.50): vocal line versus instrumental figuration, in one number.

These numbers say what differs, not why or which is better. Instrumentation, genre and annotators all differ; the caveats column on each metric page says so.

2. Beethoven's metronome — how far below the marks do 36 conductors play?

Beethoven left metronome numbers for Symphonies 1–8, and for two centuries they have been called too fast. Martín-Castro and Ucar (2020) extracted tempi from 1,332 tracks by 36 conductors. Re-aggregating their open data (Symphony 9 excluded, as in the paper):

Romantic conductors sit 12.7 beats a minute below the marks at the median; even period-instrument conductors are 6 below. No conductor's median is faster than the marks. Movement by movement, though, 152 of 1,188 readings (12.8%) run faster — the distribution leans slow; not every performance is slow. The authors note that this gap of about 13 bpm matches the height of the weight on a Maelzel metronome of the period (15 mm on its scale = 12 bpm) and suggest Beethoven misread the scale. Whatever one makes of that reading, the fact that all 36 conductors are slower on average is in the data.

3. How differently do pianists play the same piece?

ASAP aligns 169 performances of 59 Well-Tempered Clavier pieces and 259 performances of 56 Beethoven sonata movements to their scores, beat by beat (12 of the 271 Beethoven performances whose beat counts do not match the score are left out). The performers are International Piano-e-Competition entrants.

  • Across-performer tempo spread (median CV over pieces with two or more performances: 40 Bach, 33 Beethoven): Bach 0.064, Beethoven 0.047. The SD of a piece's mean tempi is 5–6% of the mean — competition pianists agree on tempo more than one might expect.
  • Beat-to-beat tempo change (rubato): Bach 0.042, Beethoven 0.078. More stretching per beat in Beethoven.
  • Dynamic spread (velocity SD): Bach 11.3, Beethoven 16.6. Markedly wider dynamics in Beethoven.

4. 180 years of repertoire — the New York Philharmonic

15,006 programs are open from the first concert on 7 December 1842 (it opened with Beethoven's Fifth) to the 2025-26 season (15,027 including the season in progress; the archive also holds programs of related bodies such as the New York Symphony and the Stadium concerts, which are included). The most performed work overall is Handel, George Frideric's MESSIAH (1,239 — counted as work entries × concerts, partial performances included); among Bach and Beethoven it is SYMPHONY NO. 5 IN C MINOR, OP.67, 633 times, rank 5 overall.

5. Limits and next steps

What is missing matters as much as what is here. The Beethoven symphonies have performance metrics but no score metrics (no open machine-readable full scores). Bach's performance data is piano only; the change in recorded durations around the period-instrument movement is not yet measured. The repertoire series is one orchestra. The caveats on each metric page and the long-term list under Method & sources record those gaps.
