A history of harmony

From late 16th-century keyboard music and madrigals to a jazz dance suite of 1931: forty collections analysed under one harmonic annotation standard, set in date order to see how the use of chords and notes changed.

1. The data

DCML's Distant Listening Corpus (Hentschel et al. 2025) holds 40 collections of scores: 1,326 pieces and movements, 117,831 bars. 1,283 of the pieces carry Roman-numeral harmony labels added under one standard; chord metrics are counted on those 1,283, note metrics on all pieces. Five of them (two Bach, two Beethoven, Mozart) already feed this site's score metrics; 35 are new. By genre: 26 keyboard, 6 chamber or solo, 8 vocal. There are no orchestral scores; the 'Wagner overtures' are piano arrangements and are counted as keyboard.

The sample was not drawn to represent its periods. It is the set of collections that researchers annotated, so keyboard music dominates and each period has only a few composers. Read the charts below as 'how these collections change', not as a history of all music.

2. Across the periods

Each dot is a collection's median over its pieces. Collections are grouped into 50-year periods by composition year, and the black line joins the medians of those collection medians, one vote per collection; periods with fewer than 2 collections are left off the line. The dashed blue line uses keyboard collections only. Hover over a dot for its collection. Note metrics leave out grace notes and the tied continuations of notes.

  • Seventh-chord ratio rises from 0.045 in the 1600s period to 0.426 in the 1900s. Between neighbouring periods on the line it goes up 5 times out of 5, and 4 out of 4 on the keyboard-only line.
  • Chromatic ratio is 0.086 in the 1600s and 0.198 in the 1900s, with a wide spread among the 20th-century collections.
  • Applied-chord ratio does not move in one direction.

3. What the annotation adds

Chord metrics count harmony labels that people added. One standard still leaves room for judgment. On this site the Modulation rate has a median of 0 in 9 collections (Sweelinck, Frescobaldi, Schütz, Handel, Clara Schumann, Mahler, Poulenc, Rachmaninoff, Schulhoff). These collections do carry local-key labels, but most of their pieces stay in one key. The value depends heavily on the length and form of the pieces, and whether a brief move to another key is labelled as a local key or as applied chords is the annotator's call. So modulation rate and cadence density stay out of the period charts and appear only in the collection table below.

The collections from 1562 to 1650 predate the settled major–minor system. Writing their chords as Roman numerals does not guarantee that a chord of the same name does the same job as in later music, so read the chord metrics for this period on their own. Metrics counted straight from the notes, such as the chromatic ratio and pitch-class entropy, are not affected.

4. Clara and Robert Schumann
