Pitch classes and chord progressions

A movement summarized not by one number but by vectors and matrices. The vector is the distribution over the twelve pitch classes and its Fourier coefficients; the matrices count where chord roots go from where, and picture how alike the measures of a movement are. A pitch class is a note name regardless of octave: middle C and the C above it are the same pitch class.

1. Pitch-class distribution and its Fourier coefficients

For each movement, the share of sounding time on each of the twelve pitch classes gives twelve numbers. Their discrete Fourier transform gives, for k = 1 to 6, how strong a pattern repeating k times around the circle is. The magnitudes lie between 0 and 1 and do not change under transposition. The figure shows, for each coefficient, a set of notes that makes it large.

Collection medians follow (number of movements in parentheses). The median of the fifth coefficient runs from 0.29 (Liszt · Années de pèlerinage) to 0.53 (Bach · Chorales). It is lower for longer movements, though (rank correlation with movement length −0.58), so it is not published as a metric, only here and in the CSV (section 4). The first coefficient is small and much the same everywhere (collection medians 0.03 to 0.08), which fits the fact that the seven notes of a diatonic scale are spread evenly around the chromatic circle (0.04 when used equally).

2. Root-transition matrices

In the 19 collections with harmony labels, 131,710 pairs of successive chords in the same local key whose root changes were counted. Rows are the root of the first chord, columns that of the second, as scale degrees from the local tonic in circle-of-fifths order (the tonic, 1, in the middle; 5, 2, 6 … to the right; 4, ♭7, ♭3 … to the left). Colour and number give the share within the row (%); n at the right is the number of transitions in the row. Rows with fewer than 30 transitions are drawn faint. The cell just left of the diagonal is a falling fifth. In minor keys the natural 3rd, 6th and 7th degrees are at ♭3, ♭6, ♭7. The line of fifths is folded to twelve cells, so enharmonic roots such as ♯1 and ♭2 share a cell; 36 transitions whose two roots fold onto one cell were left out.

Transition entropy

One number for each matrix. For each row, the entropy of the next root given the first, divided by log₂11 (the normalization Moss et al. 2019 apply to chord symbols, applied here to roots); the rows are then averaged with their frequencies as weights (the weighted mean is this site's addition). 0 means the next root is always determined; 1 means the other eleven are equally likely. Even from the same data, entropy estimates come out lower when fewer transitions are drawn, so each value is the mean of 200 draws of 400 transitions per collection and mode. These are 0.003 to 0.057 below the values from all transitions. The 3 cells with fewer than 400 transitions are blank. Values run from 0.401 (Pleyel · String quartets, major) to 0.715 (Grieg · Lyric Pieces, minor). Of the 16 collections with both values, minor is higher in 14. Intervals are 95% intervals from 200 bootstrap resamples of movements (clustering by work or annotator is not taken into account); where some resamples had fewer than 400 transitions the interval is not given. The intervals show how much each value varies; they are not a test of the difference between two collections. Collections whose intervals overlap widely are not ranked here.

A single movement has too few transitions to fill a 12 × 12 matrix (median 122 per movement), so the movement-level metric root-motion variety uses only the distribution over the eleven intervals by which the root moves.

3. One movement at a time

57 sample movements: for each collection, the one whose number of key changes is closest to the collection median, the one with the most, and the one with the fewest (the rule of the key maps on the 'Romantic pieces' page; the chorales are too short and are left out).

  • The strip on top shows the annotated local keys: grey for the home key (and its relative), orange for keys on the sharp side, blue for the flat side, stronger the further away.
  • The square is the self-similarity matrix. Both axes are measures; the stronger a cell's colour, the more alike the pitch-class distributions of the two measures (cosine similarity; 0.5 or less is left blank, 1 is strongest). On the diagonal each measure with notes is compared with itself, so it is strongest. Strong lines parallel to the diagonal suggest passages that repeat; strong squares are stretches that use similar notes. Two measures with the same distribution can still differ in the order of notes and in rhythm, so whether a passage really repeats has to be checked in the score. A measure with only rests is an empty line, diagonal included.
  • The lines below give note density and the chromatic ratio for each 4-bar window, each scaled to its own maximum.

The matrix uses pitch-class distributions that are not transposed to the local key (octaves are ignored). A theme that returns in another key usually looks less alike, so read it with the strip. Repeats are not unfolded. The order of notes within a measure, rhythm and register are not taken into account (note lengths serve only as weights).

4. Which summaries became metrics

Five movement-level numbers from this page were candidates for metrics, with three conditions fixed before computing: (a) |Spearman rank correlation| with log movement length at most 0.5 (for root-motion variety, also with the number of root changes); (b) |Pearson correlation| with each related existing metric at most 0.9; (c) blank for at most 30% of movements. 2 of the five met the conditions; the other three move one way as movements get longer. Of the two that passed, only root-motion variety is published as a metric. Semitone clustering was withheld after computing: no reading can be given for its values in tonal music (collection medians 0.03 to 0.08), and it hardly correlates with any existing metric or with the year composed, so these data cannot tell new information from noise. The four that are not metrics appear only here and in the CSV.

Variation inside a movement

Each movement is cut into 4-bar windows (no overlap; an incomplete last window is dropped). For each window the chromatic ratio and note density are computed; then the standard deviation of the windows' chromatic ratio and the coefficient of variation (SD ÷ mean) of their density. Movements with fewer than four complete windows are blank (930 movements have a value; most chorales are under 16 measures). Both grow with movement length (rank correlations 0.61 and 0.71), so the collection medians below also reflect how long each collection's movements are. Collections with fewer than five movements with a value are blank.
